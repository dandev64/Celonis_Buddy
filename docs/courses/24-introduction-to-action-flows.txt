Defining Action Flows: The "What" of Automation

Action Flows are the transactional engine of the Celonis Platform.




Architecturally, Action Flows are a specialized iPaaS (Integration Platform as a Service) built directly into Celonis. They provide a secure, high-speed bridge between your process intelligence and your tech stack. By using a low-code interface, they remove the need for complex custom coding, allowing you to turn insights into system-level reality in seconds.

Why "Transactional"?

In the world of automation, "transactional" refers to discrete, short-burst units of work. Action Flows are built for precision and speed, handling tasks such as:

Automatically updating specific records in an ERP.
Sending a targeted alert to a team member based on a live condition.
Triggering a bot to download a specific document.




Three Ways to Close the Loop

Action Flows aren't just for "autopilot" automation; they are flexible enough to support various business strategies. Depending on the complexity of the task, you will typically build one of three types of flows:

Full Automation
Human-in-the-Loop
Intelligent Reporting

The "Awareness" approach. The flow gathers data from multiple sources to create a specialized report, an email summary, or a Jira ticket to alert a team that action is required.

Whether fully automated or guided, Action Flows are often the hidden logic powering the "Action buttons" your business users interact with in their daily Celonis Views.

Action Flows vs. Process Orchestrations

As you explore the Celonis Platform, you will encounter two primary ways to drive action: Action Flows and the Orchestration Engine (referred to in Studio as a Process Orchestration asset). While they often work together, they serve different purposes.

The easiest way to understand the difference is through the analogy of an Orchestra 🎷:

CATEGORY	ACTION FLOW	PROCESS ORCHESTRATION
Analogy	

The Musician

Think of an Action Flow as a specialized musician. A violinist is an expert at one specific task: playing the violin. They are fast, precise, and transactional.

	

The Conductor

The Orchestration Engine (or Process Orchestration asset in Studio) is the Conductor. The Conductor doesn’t play an instrument; instead, they manage the entire performance. They know which musician should play and when.


State	

Stateless

When the song ends, the musician doesn't need to "remember" the notes to perform the next task.

	

Stateful

The Conductor must remember the "state" of the entire symphony—where we are in the music and what happens next.


Focus	

Action Flows are built for Task Automation. They execute a specific system update or notification in seconds and then finish their job.

	

The Orchestration Engine is built, as the name implies, for Process Orchestration. It manages long-running processes that span days or weeks, coordinating multiple Action Flows and human tasks over time.

While it is important to understand how these two engines complement each other, this course focuses exclusively on Action Flows. If you are completing this as part of the "Build Action Flows" training track, you will find a deeper dive into the relationship between these assets in a later asset.

If you're eager to learn all the details about the "Conductor", you can explore the "Orchestration Engine Fundamentals" course in the Academy at any time.

Automation and Integration

Beyond simple alerts, Action Flows serve as a professional-grade Integration Platform (iPaaS). In this role, they act as the connective tissue between disparate software systems, ensuring that data moves seamlessly across your entire organization.

The Value: Data Integrity and Accuracy

When systems are disconnected, humans are often forced into manual data reconciliation—the tedious task of moving information between windows and systems by hand. This is not only slow but introduces a significant risk of human error. By automating these integrations, you ensure that every system has the same "single source of truth," keeping your process digital twin perfectly in sync.

Key Examples:

System-to-System Synchronization
Hybrid Connectivity (The On-Prem Agent)
Sustainability & ESG Enrichment

Pro Tip: Universal Connectivity

While there are thousands of pre-made modules for major platforms, you can connect to any modern software using the HTTP module. If a system has an available API, Action Flows can communicate with it.

Synchronizing systems ensures your data is accurate across the board, but the real magic happens when you apply Process Intelligence to those systems. On the next page, we’ll see how Action Flows move beyond simple data movement to solve high-impact, process-specific challenges like credit blocks and duplicate payments.

Use Case Introduction

Before we open Celonis Studio and create our first Action Flow, let’s define exactly what you are going to build. For your first Action Flow, we will keep it simple and focused on the core "Trigger and Action" logic.

The Scenario: Your AI Sports Consultant

Throughout this chapter, we will build a single, cohesive Action Flow from scratch. To make this practical, we are going to solve a common dilemma: "Should I exercise outside today?"

The Mission

You are building an AI Sports Consultant. This automation will, in a first step, look up the weather in a dedicated location specified by you. It won't stop there, though; it will analyze the data and give you a personalized recommendation on which sport to pursue based on your own preferences and the current conditions in your city.

How it works

We will build this in four logical stages:

The Trigger: You provide the name of the city you are in.
The Data: The flow fetches live weather data (e.g., temperature, rain, etc.) for that city.
The Intelligence: An AI module will process that weather data alongside your personal sports preferences to "think" of the best recommendation.
The Action: The Action Flow sends you a notification with the final verdict.
Why this Use Case?

This scenario covers the three pillars of every professional Action Flow: Data Integration, Logic/Reasoning, and Automated Action. Mastering these here will allow you to build much more complex business automations—like AI-driven credit checks or automated procurement agents—later in your career.

Dynamic Mapping and Inputs [03:30]
Set up a Location Input

To move from a static test to a dynamic automation, we first need to define an entry point for our data. This is done through the Inputs panel (found in the top menu bar), which creates a dedicated field for a user to provide information — like a city name — before the flow starts.

Creating the Input Parameter
Open the Inputs Panel: Click the Inputs tab in the top menu bar of the Action Flow editor.
Add a New Parameter: Click + Add item to create your first input.
Configure the new input's name: Name it, e.g., City or Location.
Type: Select Text from the dropdown menu.
Keep "No" for the "Required" setting - We will see later why. 
Save: Click the Save button within the panel to finalize the input.

The Bigger Picture: Connecting to the Business

While we are creating this input manually today, think of this as a doorway. In a real business scenario, this input is what allows your Action Flow to communicate with the rest of the Celonis platform.

For example, a business user might click a button in a Celonis View (i.e., their dashboard) to trigger this flow. When they do, the specific data they are looking at—like a vendor's location or a shipping destination—is automatically passed into this input. By setting this up now, you are building an automation that can be triggered directly from the heart of the business process.

The Vending Machine Analogy

Think of an Action Flow like a vending machine. To make it work, the machine needs an Input—the code you type into the keypad (like "A1").

Without a keypad, the machine would be "hard-coded" to only ever give you the same bag of chips. By adding an Input, you are giving your flow a Keypad. This allows the user to tell the flow exactly which "item" (in our case, which City) they want it to process without having to take the machine apart and rewire it every time.

Now that you have your "keypad" (the Input) set up, you need to tell the Weather module to listen to it. Let's do this next.

Dynamic Mapping and Inputs [03:30]
Map the Input

What we are going to do now is replace the so far static location value in the weather module with our new input variable. This process is in the Action Flows world, usually referred to as Mapping. Instead of the module looking at a static word like "Munich," it will now look at whatever value is stored in your City input variable.

How to Map the Input
Open the Weather Module: Click on your Get current weather module to open its settings.
Clear the Old Data: Delete the city name you typed in earlier from the City field.

Find your Variable: Click into the now-empty City field. A mapping panel will slide out. Look for the section labeled Action Flow Inputs, which you'll find inside "Custom and system variables".

Insert the Variable: Click on your City variable to map it to the Weather module's city field. It will somewhat look like a "data pill", what you see in the city field now.

What is a data pill?

The "pill" is a visual representation of dynamic data. Whenever you see one, it means the Action Flow will plug in a value at the moment the flow runs. It’s no longer a fixed instruction; it’s a dynamic placeholder.

Save and Re-Run the Flow

Once the pill is in place, click OK to close the module settings and hit the Save button in the top-left menu bar. Your Weather module is now officially "wired" to your input.

Now it’s time to test the "keypad." By running the flow now, you will see how the input you created actually controls the Weather module. Click once again "Run once". Unlike before, the flow won't start immediately. A window will appear asking for your City input.

Enter your desired city and click "OK".

As before, open the output and inspect the results. Everything will still look the same as before, BUT…

Why this is a Milestone

You have just transitioned from a fixed script to a functional app. While it might seem simple for a single module, think about the bigger picture:

Imagine an Action Flow with 10 or 20 different modules—all of which need to know the name of the city to do their jobs. Without an Input, you would have to manually open every single module and type the city name 20 times (and possibly, change it).

By using an Input, you create a single source of truth. You type the city name once at the start, and the data pills automatically distribute that value across every module in your flow. This doesn't just save time; it ensures your automation is consistent, error-proof, and professional.

Recap: The Action Flow's Goal

Now that we have our data source (Weather) and our user input (City), it’s time to move into the "Intelligence" phase of our build. We aren't just going to report the temperature; we are going to use Generative AI to act as a personal sports consultant.

The Two-Phase Build

To create a truly smart automation, we are structuring this flow in two distinct phases:

Phase 1: Data Gathering (Complete): We’ve already set up the flow to ask for a city and fetch the local conditions. This provides the "Facts."
Phase 2: AI Reasoning (Next): We will add a Create AI Prompt module. This module will take the weather and combine it with personal preferences (e.g., I love running, but I don't like the rain) to generate a tailor-made sports recommendation.
The Goal

Instead of a message saying "It's 15°C and raining," our flow will produce a personal recommendation like: "Since it's raining in London and you prefer indoor activities, why not skip the run and head to the local climbing gym today?"

Why use AI in Action Flows?

In a business context, this is one way of how you can handle "unstructured" decisions. While a standard module can tell you an invoice is late, an AI module can look at the history of that vendor and draft a specific, polite yet firm email based on the severity of the delay. We are using the "Sports Coach" example to master this exact logic.

Basic Error Handling [07:00]
Identify the Error Type

Experiencing your first failing Action Flow is never easy… But: in the world of automation, it's actually a vital point of your journey. An error isn't a broken flow—it's the platform telling you exactly where your logic needs a safety net. Before we fix the flow, we need to understand what went wrong.

The "Bundle Validation Error"

If you click on your failed execution, you will likely see a Bundle Validation Error. This is the platform's way of saying: "I tried to process this step, but a required piece of data was missing or invalid." In our case, because the flow ran on a schedule, the "City" input was empty, and the Weather module didn't know how to proceed.

Remember the Input's "Required" setting from before? If that was set to "Yes", our Action Flow would also have failed, but due to an initialization error.




The Action Flow "Error Gallery"

While Bundle Validation is our current hurdle, here are the other "usual suspects" you’ll meet as an architect:

Connection & Authentication Errors: Your "key" to a service (like an API key or password) has expired or changed.
Data Type Mismatches: You tried to give a module a word when it specifically asked for a number (e.g., trying to calculate the temperature of "Sunny").
Rate Limit Errors: You are asking a service for data too quickly, and it has temporarily blocked you to protect its servers.

Now that we’ve unmasked our error, it’s time to move from diagnosis to resolution. While the 'Rescue Path' we are about to build is a powerful fix for this specific hurdle, keep in mind that we are just scratching the surface. In the wider world of Action Flows, error handling is a deep discipline—ranging from complex data filtering to 'self-healing' logic. 

Check out our course "Handle Errors in Action Flows" to dive into the deeper waters of error handling. This course is also part of the "Build Action Flows" training track.

Rescuing the Flow with Error Routes

We've successfully identified the cause of our failing Action Flow execution. Rather than letting the flow simply stop, we can build an Error Handling Route that provides the missing data and keeps the Action Flow moving.

What is an Error Handling Route?

An Error Handling Route is a specialized path that only activates if a module fails. It allows the flow to "catch" the error and try a different approach instead of crashing. An error handling route is always specific to a single module in your Action Flow, not the entire thing.

Implementing the Error Handling Route

After consultation with the user of our sports recommendation, we decided that if the input is missing, we go for the hometown of the user as a default. 

🤔 We cannot just add a "default" value to our location input. Even if this may be the first intuition. 

Therefore, we need a second backup "Get current weather" module. This backup module will be the first of the error-handling route; this time with a hard-coded location (just as we had set it up in the beginning).

An Error Handling Route can be added to any module and is triggered if that particular module cannot proceed due to an error. To add and define it:

Add the handler: Start by right-clicking the module that you want to add the error handling route to; in this case, it's our (existing) "Get current weather" module. Select "Add error handler".
Add backup module: On this new route, add a second Get current weather module. Instead of using a data pill, hardcode your home city (e.g., "Munich").
Insert the Resume directive: After your backup weather module, add the Resume directive.

The Resume directive is one of many error-handling directives available in Action Flows.

It replaces the module output with a substitute output when an error happens.
It is the "bridge" that takes you back to the main path.
In the Resume directive's settings, you must provide the "Expected" data for the next modules: map the Temperature and Condition from your backup weather module here.

If you don't want to wait for the dedicated course, check out the documentation to learn more about the Resume and other error-handling directives.
