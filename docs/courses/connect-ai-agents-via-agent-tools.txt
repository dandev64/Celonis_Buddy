A Glimpse at our Use Case

We just made a promise: to turn raw backend data into answers you can rely on. To see what that means in practice, we'll keep returning to one example from procurement and sustainability - a space where trusted, governed context clearly pays off.

Here is the problem:

A company wants to measure the carbon footprint of everything it buys - its "Purchased Goods and Services" emissions. Every purchase sits in a table in the company's ERP system. The emission factors needed to calculate that footprint sit in another table - the Material Table.

Optional deep dive: Carbon Accounting 101




For a purchase to count toward total emissions, it has to be connected to the Material Table record that carries its emission factor. When that link exists, the calculation happens automatically. When it doesn't, the purchase carries no emissions - and barely anyone notices.

Matching is easy when a purchase carries a material number, a code that points straight to the Material Table. Most catalogue orders work this way.

It breaks with off-catalogue orders. Instead of a material number, someone types a short free-text description - a few words, in their own style. It gets approved and bought; the business gets what it needs. But free text has no material number, so it never gets matched. When the report is pulled, that line has nothing to calculate from. The spend is real and the emissions are real, but the report can't see them. Multiply that across thousands of purchases, and the report has a gap in it.

How an AI agent can help

In theory, a human could do it by hand. Someone...

reads each free-text line,
works out what was actually bought,
finds the closest material in the Material Table, and
applies its emission factor.

Right kind of thinking - but across thousands of lines it's slow, repetitive, and easy to get wrong halfway down the list. So in practice it rarely gets done, and the hole in the report stays.

An AI agent can do the same reasoning, just faster and more consistently.

It reads those free-text lines, matches each one to the most likely material, and applies that material's emission factor - producing an educated estimate of the emissions that were missing. It won't claim to have measured them; it's a best guess, grounded in real material and emission data. And where it can't find enough to go on, it doesn't force a match - it flags that line for a human to verify or complete, so judgement is spent only where it's actually needed.

What is an AI Agent?

We are going to talk about AI agents a lot in this course - actually, we've already started - so let's make sure we're on the same page about what they are. And especially distinguish them from AI Chatbots.

A chatbot answers a question and stops. An AI agent goes further: it pursues a goal. The simplest working definition:

An AI agent is a software system that perceives its context, reasons over it, and can take actions on its own to achieve a goal.

Three characteristics are what turn a plain language model into an agent:

Goal-oriented autonomy - it works toward an objective and can decide its own next steps, rather than waiting for instructions at every turn.
Reasoning capability - it interprets the situation and chooses what to do, instead of following a fixed script.
Tool-enabled action - it can use tools to reach beyond conversation: query data, call a system, or trigger a process.




An important rationale in that definition: an agent is only as good as the context it perceives. Reasoning over thin or messy information produces confident but wrong decisions. Give an agent rich, structured, trustworthy context about how your business actually runs, and its reasoning improves dramatically. That single idea - agents need good context - is the thread running through this entire course, and it is exactly what the Celonis platform is built to provide.

What this could look like in practice

Think back to that free-text requisition. An AI agent picks up a vague line - say, "Apple Blend 1L" typed straight into the box - and goes to work. It perceives the request, reasons about which catalog material it actually refers to, looks up that material's details, and surfaces something nobody could see before: the carbon footprint behind the purchase. Perceive, reason, act - all in service of one goal: turn an invisible, free-text request into a measured one. That is an agent, and it is very close to the one you will build in this course.

💭 Notice the shift from the chatbot: the agent did not just answer a question, it pursued an outcome across several steps - interpret, look up, calculate, explain. 

Where in your own work do you make a "figure out what they meant, then dig up the answer" call that an agent could take on?

Now that we share a definition, the natural next question is: how much freedom should an agent have to act? That is where autonomy comes in.

Modes of AI Agent Autonomy

Before you let an agent loose on your operations, you have one big decision to make: how much freedom does it get? It is not all-or-nothing. Agent autonomy sits on a spectrum, and where you land shapes how you design, secure, and govern the agent. Here are the three modes you will hear about most.

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL)
Human-on-the-Loop (HOTL)
Human-out-of-the-Loop (HOOTL)

The agent acts purely as an analytical assistant, surfacing insights or recommendations. It cannot execute decisions or write back to any system without direct, manual human approval for every step.




Keep this spectrum in mind. When you design your own agent, choosing its autonomy level will be one of the fundamental decisions you make - and the level you choose isn't permanent. Different agents will sit at different points on the spectrum, and a single agent's level often shifts over time. In practice, many teams start an agent with tight human oversight and grant it more autonomy as it earns trust. Advances in technology and changes in business rules push in the same direction, making this an iterative design process - just like anything in the software world.

💭 Quick gut check: for a use case you have in mind, which mode feels right today, and what would have to be true before you would trust the next level up?

Introduction to the Model Context Protocol [10:00]
The Anthropic Standard & Core Primitives

Before you connect an AI agent to Celonis, it helps to understand the shared language they use to talk to each other. That language is the Model Context Protocol, or MCP.

MCP is an open standard, originally introduced and open-sourced by Anthropic. Its job is simple but powerful: give any AI application a single, consistent way to connect to external data and tools. Before MCP, every connection between a model and a piece of software had to be custom-built. MCP replaces that tangle of one-off integrations with one shared standard.

💡 Quick analogy. If APIs are like dozens of differently shaped power sockets, MCP is the single universal plug that fits them all. Build to the standard once, and your agent can connect to anything that speaks MCP.

The three building blocks of MCP

The protocol defines three core primitives. Each answers a different question about how an agent works with an external system.

Tools - The Agent's Toolkit 🔧
Resources - The Agent's Library 📖
Prompts - The Agent's Blueprint 💬

For now, the key takeaway: Celonis builds on this open standard, and it focuses deliberately on Tools. We will unpack exactly why later in the course.

Comparing MCP to traditional APIs

APIs have connected software to software for decades, and they are not going away. But reasoning models work differently from traditional programs, and that changes what a good connection looks like.

A classic API expects rigid, exact inputs. Send the wrong parameter type or miss a required field, and the call simply fails. That is fine for deterministic systems. But an AI agent reasons in natural language and decides what to do on the fly, so it needs an interface it can read, understand, and adapt to.

This is where MCP shines. Instead of hard-coded calls, MCP exposes tools with self-describing schemas. The agent reads what each tool does and figures out how to call it. 

One way to put it: a traditional API connects software to software; MCP connects AI to software.

FEATURE	TRADITIONAL APIS (REST / SOAP)	MODEL CONTEXT PROTOCOL (MCP)
Designed for	Deterministic software systems with fixed logic.	Reasoning LLMs that interpret context and decide.

What is Agent Tools? 


Celonis Agent Tools is a Studio asset that hands an external AI agent a curated set of tools - securely, over the open Model Context Protocol (MCP) - so the agent can tap into your Celonis process context: search your Knowledge Models, pull real-time process metrics, and even trigger actions.

A simple way to remember it: with Process Copilot and ChatAPI, Celonis brings the brain. With Agent Tools, you bring your own brain and Celonis grounds it in operational truth.

Designing your AI Agent with Intention

Once you know you want to build an AI Agent, it is tempting to jump straight into configuration. Resist that urge for a moment. The hardest part of building a useful agent is often not only technical; it is design.

We could just give you a step-by-step configuration list for both the platform where you set up the agent, as well as the steps to take in Celonis where you set up the Agent Tools. That would be relatively easy. But in the end, you would not be able to tell why we chose a certain set of tools. And that's the real trick. 

You need to actively decide what your agent is for, to know which tools to give it or where to draw its boundaries. This way, you make sure to come up with a well-scoped agent, which is predictable and reliable.

Good agent design is its own soft skill.

To get started with your Agent Design process, here are a couple of reflective questions that can help you to get the ball rolling: 

Purpose: What single job should this agent do well - in one sentence?
Scope: What is explicitly out of scope? Narrow beats broad, especially when it comes to AI.
Tools: Which - in this case, Celonis - tools are strictly necessary for that job?
Guardrails: Should the agent only read and recommend, or also act and write back? (Remember the autonomy spectrum from earlier.)

And yes, actually writing down your answers, discussing them with your team, and iterating on them is all part of a good practice to come up with an Agent Design that is purposeful, targeted and will yield the desired outcomes. Maybe you even come up with some sort of "Agent Model Canvas"?

💡 Fewer tools, sharper agent. 

Later, in the Celonis configuration screen of Agent Tools, you will even be warned when you select more than three tools. Every extra tool adds ambiguity to the agent's decision-making.

Don't worry; we won't make you go through this exercise of answering the questions for our training use case. Here is how we answered those four questions for you:

Purpose: reveal the carbon footprint hiding in free-text purchase requisitions.
Scope: read and reveal only - match the requests, recover the factors, surface the hotspots. No rerouting spend, no writing anything back.
Tools: just the couple of read tools it needs to find a material and its emission factor - nothing more.
Guardrails: strictly read-only. The agent informs; a human decides.

Notice how tight that is. We are keeping the build deliberately simple - the cleverness lives in how the agent reasons over a few good tools, not in piling on more.

Opening the Tool Box

On the next pages, you will create your own Agent Tools asset in Celonis Studio and select its tools. Let's open the toolbox, so you know what you are choosing from.

Agent Tools may be grouped into two clusters (theoretical; nothing you would find in the UI like that), based on what you let the agent do:

Find and read (the usual starting point)

These are tools that let the agent search and retrieve trusted context. Many "chat with your process" agents run on a small, deliberate set of read tools: Load Data (pull the specific data to reason over), Search Data (find relevant items in your Knowledge Models from a search term), and Get Knowledge Model (retrieve the Knowledge Model itself as grounding). A fourth, Search Metadata, plays a supporting role - helping the agent discover what is available. Together, these cover the large majority of analytical use cases.

While these tools sound similar - and to a certain extent are similar - this may also be the point where it gets tricky to choose which one you actually need. While we put all of them into the "Find and read" bucket, let's still look at them separately:

Load Data - you know what you want
Search Data - describe what you want
Search Metadata - discover what exists
Get Knowledge Model - as the name suggests

A classic pattern is search, then load: use Search Data to find the right record from a vague description, then Load Data to pull its full details.

💡 A note on Semantic search.

Semantic search matches on meaning and is powered by a vector store, which is still being rolled out and requires an extra setup. You will therefore not be able to test it in the training environment.

Act and automate

Tools that let the agent do something in your operations rather than just report on them - for example, Trigger Action Flow to kick off an existing Action Flow, or the Orchestration Engine tools to start and resume a long-running process. These move the agent up the autonomy spectrum, from advising to acting, which is also why this is where you will want tighter guardrails and, often, a human in the loop.

This is also how an agent writes back into Celonis and closes the loop. There is no separate write-back tool: instead, the Action Flow the agent triggers can include a Celonis module that writes its decision or outcome back to the source, so the next analysis reflects what the agent just did.




Whichever tools you choose, most of them can be set up in one of two ways: in an intelligent or custom configuration, depending on your needs:

Intelligent Configuration
Custom configuration

The tool comes ready to use: you add it and go, no bespoke setup required. It is the fastest path and the right default for standard find-and-read needs - for example, letting the agent load data on demand. Not every tool offers this, but "Load Data" is one of them.

Worth noting: it might be a design choice of yours to mix the two configuration types, i.e. set up two tools of the same kind; one in intelligent configuration, one in custom configuration for a specific job.

💡 Less is more. Add a fourth tool and Studio warns you directly: "Selecting too many tools can negatively impact your accuracy." There is good reason for that - every extra tool gives the agent one more thing to second-guess. A focused toolset makes for a sharper, more predictable agent. This is where the scoping from "Designing your AI Agent with Intention" pays off. (That said, sometimes a fourth tool really is the right call - we will come back to exactly that when we build.)

In this course, we will stick to the find-and-read tools, which are all an agent needs to answer questions about your process emissions.

From Question to Toolset

You have scoped the agent: its purpose, its boundaries, its read-only guardrails. Now let's turn that scope into an actual set of Agent Tools. As a starting point, it is often helpful to think through how you would do the agent's job yourself - by hand, step by step, the way you might in a spreadsheet.

Picture working through it manually. Every time you stop to look something up - the confirmed figures, the free-text lines, a matching material, its emission factor - that is a moment the agent will need a tool. Every time it is just arithmetic or judgement, the agent needs no tool at all. Keep that split in mind.

In a nutshell, you take the agent's future work, break it into steps, and ask the following for each step:

a) does this step need trusted data from Celonis? That is a tool.

b) or is it reasoning the model can do on its own? That is no tool.

Let's apply this to our use case:

"What's the carbon footprint of our cheese-related purchases, accounting for both confirmed and hidden emissions?"

STEP THE AGENT TAKES	NEEDS TRUSTED CELONIS DATA?	TOOL?
Get the confirmed emissions for the catalogue-matched cheese lines - a governed KPI the agent asks Celonis for rather than working out.	Yes	get_emissions
Get the free-text cheese lines and their total spend - the lines the KPI cannot see.	Yes	get_free_text_spend
Match each free-text description to the most likely catalogued material - a fuzzy lookup, not an exact one.	Yes	search_emission_factor
Recover that material's emission factor.	Yes	get_emission_factor
Estimate the hidden emissions and add them to the confirmed figure - one multiplication (spend × factor), one addition.	No - pure arithmetic on what the tools returned	No tool
Explain the result - confirmed, estimated, total, and the caveat that the hidden part is a best guess.	No - reasoning	No tool

Four steps need data; two are pure reasoning. So our toolset is four tools: get_emissions, get_free_text_spend, search_emission_factor, and get_emission_factor. Notice we did not choose them - the question did.

Remember Studio warns you add more than three tools? We land on four on purpose. "Why" is something we will answer throughout the course. For now keep in mind: There is no single right answer; tool count is a design choice you make against your question.

Authentication & Permission

A published Agent Tools asset is not reachable until something is allowed to reach it. Two things make that happen: credentials, so a client can prove who it is, and permission, so it is actually allowed to use the asset.

💡 For this course, this is already done for you. In our training environment, the OAuth connection and all required permissions have been set up in the background. You do not need to create or grant anything on this page - just read it to understand the concept for when you set things up in your own productive environment, then move straight on to the hands-on exercise. 

One detail worth knowing: the client we set up for you uses the Authorization Code grant type, because that is what is required to connect an Agent Tools asset to Microsoft Copilot Studio - the platform you will (optionally) use later in this course.

Also, don't forget you can find all the info about OAuth 2.0 setup on Celonis Developer Docs!

Credentials: OAuth 2.0

Agent Tools uses OAuth 2.0 to authenticate clients - the secure, standard way to connect an external agent. In your own environment, you would create an OAuth client in the Admin Settings and note its Client ID and Client Secret. OAuth offers two grant types, depending on how the agent acts:

Client Credentials - best for server-to-server connections and automated workflows, where the agent acts as itself.
Authorization Code - best when the agent acts on behalf of a person. This is the important one for governance: it carries each user's own Celonis permissions through to the agent, so a user only ever sees data they are already allowed to see. It is also the grant type required to connect to Microsoft Copilot Studio.

Either way, the client is given the scope mcp-asset.tools:execute, which is what lets it call the asset's tools.

💡 Planning to wire an Agent Tools asset up to Microsoft Copilot Studio in your own environment? Celonis has a step-by-step developer guide for exactly that: the Microsoft connector integration guide, including the Authorization Code setup it needs.

Permission: the step everyone forgets

Credentials alone are not enough. The OAuth client must also be granted the use permission on your Agent Tools asset, plus access to the Studio Space where it lives. Miss this, and every connection attempt fails - even with perfect credentials. In real projects, this is the single most common reason a connection will not authenticate.

Notice the two-part logic: who are you (credentials), and are you allowed (permission)? When a connection refuses to authenticate, the answer is almost always one of those two - usually the missing use permission.

Search Data, Load Data, and Get Knowledge Model, optionally Get Insights - all on intelligent configuration.	These let the agent find the right data, ground itself in your Knowledge Model, and reason over it. The field-proven starting recipe - it covers the large majority of "chat with your process" use cases.
The Action-taking Agent	Does not just surface a problem - it does something about it.	Reads and acts - moves you up the autonomy spectrum, so scope it tightly.	A read tool (Search Data or Load Data) plus Trigger Action Flow. 	The read tool lets the agent understand the situation; the Action Flow lets it take a concrete step, such as sending a notification or updating a record.
The Process Agent (advanced)	Participates in long-running, multi-step Celonis processes rather than answering one-off questions.	Acts over time on stateful processes - the most powerful, so reserve it deliberately.	Get Process Contexts, Execute Orchestration, and Resume Orchestration Execution, plus a read tool for context. 	These let the agent start and continue stateful processes.
The Reuse-your-Copilot Agent	Leans on a Process Copilot you have already built and tuned.	Inherits the behaviour of the Copilot you expose.	Call Process Copilot, often alongside a read tool. 	Instead of rebuilding curated prompts and filters in your AI platform, you expose your existing Copilot as a single tool - a real time-saver when the tuned logic already exists.

💡 Four rules of thumb for any toolset

Start with the smallest read set that answers the question; add tools only as the job demands.
Read and insight tools handle "tell me"; action tools handle "do it." 
Pair every action tool with the read tool(s) it needs to make a good decision.
Keep it tight: Studio warns past three tools, and more tools mean more ambiguity for the agent.

While we're on the three-tool-rule...

Look back at the agent you built in this course. We landed on four tools. Was that the only option? Not at all. We could have folded the emission-factor lookup into the tool that returns the confirmed data, for a leaner three-tool design. We didn't, on purpose: that shortcut quietly assumes every free-text line already has a catalogued counterpart - which is exactly the gap our agent exists to expose. So we spent one extra tool to find each factor the honest way, and keep the hidden-emissions estimate trustworthy.

That is the real lesson. The "right" number of tools is not a rule you follow; it is a judgment call you make against your question - and sometimes a fourth tool genuinely earns its place.

Why Tools, not Resources

Remember the three MCP primitives: Tools, Resources, and Prompts? You may have noticed that Celonis Agent Tools focuses almost entirely on one of them: Tools. That is a deliberate design choice. Here is the logic.

Why not Resources?

A Resource hands raw data to the model so it can read it directly. That works for a document or a small record. But think about the data behind our agent: hundreds of thousands of purchase-requisition lines, plus the full material catalog. Try to pass that as a Resource, and two things happen: you blow straight past the model's context window, and your token costs skyrocket. Worse, the model drowns in detail that it cannot reason over reliably.

Why Tools win

Tools flip the model. Instead of shipping raw data to the agent, the heavy computation stays on Celonis. The matching and the lookup run where the data lives, and only a clean, condensed answer travels back. 




💡 In one line: Resources would force the agent to swallow the haystack. Tools let Celonis hand it the needle.

This reframes "partial" as "purposeful." Knowing what Celonis deliberately left out, does the tools-only approach make sense to you now?
